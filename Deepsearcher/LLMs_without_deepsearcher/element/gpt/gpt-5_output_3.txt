Proposed catalyst: a high‑entropy Prussian blue analog with a mixed A‑site of Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ coordinated to the N‑end of cyanide, and a B‑site of [Fe(CN)6]3− units coordinated through the C‑end. A representative composition is (Ni, Co, Mn, Zn)3[Fe(CN)6]2·nH2O with intrinsic [Fe(CN)6] vacancies that create porosity and hydration channels. This choice delivers five transition metals in total (Ni, Co, Mn, Zn, Fe), balancing catalytic performance, structural stability, cost, and environmental considerations for the urea oxidation reaction (UOR) in alkaline media.

Why these five metals and not others
- Metals deliberately avoided: Cr and V (toxicity and environmental concerns), Ru/Ir (high cost), Mo/W (cyanide chemistry is less compatible and they raise material cost), Cu (can add activity and conductivity but is more prone to leaching under anodic alkaline conditions; excluding Cu improves corrosion resistance and environmental profile without sacrificing performance thanks to Ni–Co–Fe–Mn synergy).
- Tentative introduction of Zn2+: chosen as the environmentally benign, stable‑valence element to optimize performance via structural stabilization and corrosion resistance while keeping cost low.

Element‑by‑element rationale

1) Nickel (Ni)
- Catalytic activity: Ni is the cornerstone for UOR in alkaline media. Under anodic bias, Ni2+ converts to β/γ‑NiOOH, which provides the active oxyhydroxide surface for urea adsorption and dehydrogenation. Ni’s eg occupancy (~1) supports balanced adsorption of OH− and urea intermediates, lowering the UOR overpotential.
- Stability: The reversible Ni(OH)2/NiOOH redox couple endows good cycling stability. In the CN‑bridged framework, Ni sites are protected against rapid dissolution and over‑oxidation.
- Synthetic feasibility: Aqueous coprecipitation from cheap Ni salts (nitrates or chlorides) with ferricyanide is straightforward at room temperature; Ni integrates well in the N‑coordinated A‑site octahedra.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Ni is abundant and inexpensive compared with noble metals.
- Structural features: Ni2+ (high‑spin octahedral) fits the A‑site M–N coordination, strengthening superexchange with the B‑site Fe via CN bridges. It also tolerates A‑site disorder, supporting high configurational entropy that suppresses phase segregation.

2) Cobalt (Co)
- Catalytic activity: Co synergizes with Ni by facilitating charge transfer and oxygen species formation on the oxyhydroxide surface. Co‑containing sites help activate C–N and N–H bonds in urea and speed up the oxidative dehydrogenation steps; Ni–Co pairs commonly exhibit superior UOR kinetics to monometallic Ni.
- Stability: CoOOH/Co(OH)2 transformations are robust under alkaline anodic potentials. Dispersed Co in a high‑entropy lattice resists localized over‑oxidation and mitigates dissolution.
- Synthetic feasibility: Co2+ salts co‑precipitate cleanly into PBAs; cobalt chemistry with cyanide is well established (e.g., [Co(CN)6]3− as a B‑site option, though here Co is placed on the A‑site to maintain five unique metals total).
- Cost‑effectiveness: More costly than Fe/Mn/Zn but still low compared with noble metals; using it as one of several components reduces dosage while leveraging its kinetic benefits.
- Structural features: Co2+ in the A‑site modulates ligand field strength and adjusts the electronic structure of neighboring Ni/Mn sites via CN‑mediated superexchange, refining adsorption energies for key UOR intermediates.

3) Manganese (Mn)
- Catalytic activity: Mn offers flexible redox (Mn2+/3+/4+), which enriches the density of active redox states near the Fermi level. It promotes OH− activation and can assist in breaking N–H bonds in urea, working as an electronic “tuner” around Ni–Co sites rather than as the main active center.
- Stability: Distributed Mn within a high‑entropy lattice avoids the instability that can accompany concentrated Mn oxides. The CN framework dampens Jahn–Teller distortions of Mn3+ by averaging local environments.
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ integrates readily into A‑site octahedra through standard aqueous precipitation with ferricyanide, and can be co‑deposited onto conductive supports.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Very low cost and abundant, improving the economic profile of the catalyst.
- Structural features: Mn’s multiple valence states enhance polaronic hopping and electronic conductivity along M–N≡C–Fe linkages while increasing defect density and oxygen vacancy‑like behavior after electrochemical activation, both beneficial for mass transport and catalysis.

4) Zinc (Zn) — the stable, environmentally benign “optimizer”
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ is catalytically mild for UOR on its own, but in a high‑entropy matrix it acts as an electronic diluent that avoids excessively strong adsorption on Ni/Co active sites. This can prevent overbinding of intermediates and improve turnover. Zn also helps expose and stabilize more Ni/Co sites during electrochemical activation.
- Stability: Zn2+ has a stable d10 configuration with no Jahn–Teller distortion, which improves crystallinity, suppresses lattice strain, and minimizes defect coalescence into failure points. Zn inclusion reduces metal leaching and enhances corrosion resistance under alkaline anodic conditions.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ salts are easy to co‑precipitate; Zn reliably occupies the A‑site and tolerates high compositional disorder required for high‑entropy materials.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Very low cost and widely available; it lowers the overall materials bill.
- Structural features: Zn’s preference for octahedral N‑coordination stiffens the A‑site sublattice, helping retain the PBA framework during cycling. Its presence raises the configurational entropy without compromising environmental safety.

5) Iron (Fe) — placed on the B‑site as [Fe(CN)6]3−
- Catalytic activity: Fe in [Fe(CN)6]3− is not the primary UOR site, but it critically tunes the electronic structure of A‑site metals through strong‑field cyanide ligation. CN bridges enable π‑backbonding and superexchange that sharpen the redox response of Ni/Co/Mn during UOR, often lowering overpotential and increasing current density. Mixed Fe(III)/Fe(II) states created during activation can provide a redox buffer that prevents over‑oxidation of A‑site metals.
- Stability: The rigid Fe‑cyanide octahedra anchor the framework, resisting collapse and dissolution. Ferricyanide PBAs are well known for structural robustness and resistance to corrosion in alkaline media when immobilized on supports.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3Fe(CN)6 is inexpensive, water‑soluble, and enables rapid room‑temperature precipitation with A‑site metal salts. The process yields uniform nanoparticles or microcubes with controllable vacancy content (y<1), which introduces beneficial porosity and hydration pathways.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Fe is abundant and cheap; using it on the B‑site avoids adding costly or hazardous species.
- Structural features: The M–N≡C–Fe linkages provide long‑range electronic pathways and defect engineering possibilities. Vacancies in [Fe(CN)6] units and interstitial water form ion channels that aid OH−/urea transport and facilitate in situ transformation of surface layers to catalytically active oxyhydroxides.

How the high‑entropy design meets the target criteria
- High catalytic activity: Ni–Co are the principal UOR centers; Mn modulates redox density and adsorption energetics; Fe(CN)6 tunes electronic coupling; Zn prevents overbinding and promotes exposure/stability of active sites. The configurational disorder broadens the distribution of active motifs, increasing site density and turnover.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: CN‑bridged Fe units lock in lattice integrity; Zn suppresses lattice strain and metal leaching; dispersed Mn and Co avoid localized over‑oxidation; Ni/Co form stable oxyhydroxide layers under operation. High configurational entropy deters phase separation and grain growth.
- Low cost and environmental friendliness: All five metals are earth‑abundant and relatively benign; cobalt usage is minimized and balanced by Fe/Mn/Zn. Hazardous metals are avoided. Cyanide is strongly bound in the lattice; synthesis and use are conducted in aqueous media, and immobilization on supports prevents particle loss.
- Ease of synthesis: Simple, room‑temperature coprecipitation of equimolar Ni2+/Co2+/Mn2+/Zn2+ salts into [Fe(CN)6]3−, followed by washing and mild drying. Growth can be done directly on Ni foam or carbon cloth to achieve binder‑free electrodes. No high‑temperature processing or complex ligands are required.
- Structural features tailored for UOR: Defect‑rich PBAs with controlled [Fe(CN)6] vacancies create mesoporosity and hydration channels that increase mass transport. A‑site disorder and B‑site rigidity together enable rapid electronic percolation and robust cycling. Electrochemical pre‑activation in alkaline electrolyte transforms the outer shell to mixed‑metal oxyhydroxides while preserving the PBA core as a conductive, corrosion‑resistant scaffold.

Practical synthesis and activation notes
- Aqueous coprecipitation: Slowly add K3Fe(CN)6 to a stirred solution containing equimolar Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Zn2+ (e.g., nitrates), maintain pH ~6–7 to avoid premature hydroxide formation, age for several hours, wash thoroughly to remove free ions, and dry at ≤80 °C to preserve lattice water.
- Direct growth on supports: Perform precipitation in the presence of cleaned Ni foam or carbon cloth to seed nucleation. Alternatively, drop‑cast colloids onto supports.
- Vacancy control: Adjust metal/Fe(CN)6 ratio and ionic strength to tune [Fe(CN)6] occupancy (y), balancing conductivity, porosity, and mechanical robustness.
- Electrochemical activation: Cycle the electrode in alkaline electrolyte (e.g., 1 M KOH) prior to UOR to form a thin, highly active mixed‑metal oxyhydroxide layer while retaining the PBA backbone.

In summary, the chosen five‑metal set—Ni, Co, Mn, Zn on the A‑site with Fe on the B‑site—provides the necessary catalytic activity, structural stability, cost‑effectiveness, synthetic simplicity, environmental compatibility, and corrosion resistance. The intentional inclusion of Zn2+ as a benign, stable‑valence component is a key optimization, improving lattice integrity and long‑term durability without compromising UOR performance.
