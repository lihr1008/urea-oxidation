Proposed catalyst: a five‑metal high‑entropy Prussian blue analog with Fe on the carbon‑coordinated site and an equimolar mixture of Ni, Co, Mn, and Zn on the nitrogen‑coordinated site. A representative composition is A x(Ni0.25Co0.25Mn0.25Zn0.25) [Fe(CN)6] y·nH2O, where A is an alkali cation (K+, Na+, NH4+), y ≤ 1 controls [Fe(CN)6] vacancies, and n captures lattice water. This design leverages the strong, redox‑active hexacyanoferrate framework and the synergistic multi‑cation surface that evolves under alkaline UOR into mixed oxyhydroxides, balancing activity, stability, low cost, environmental friendliness, and corrosion resistance.

Why this framework:
- Prussian blue analogs (PBAs) are open, cyanide‑bridged networks with two distinct metal sites: the C‑bound hexacyanometallate node and the N‑bound metal node. They are easy to synthesize in water at room temperature from inexpensive salts, form high surface area microporous solids with tunable vacancy content, and present abundant redox‑active centers. Under alkaline UOR, the outer few nanometers reconstruct to mixed (Ni,Co,Fe,Mn,Zn) oxyhydroxides that are excellent UOR catalysts, while the cyanide‑bridged core maintains electronic connectivity and structural integrity.
- High‑entropy mixing of four N‑site cations raises configurational entropy, suppresses cation segregation and dissolution, and introduces beneficial local strain and defects that accelerate charge transfer and intermediate adsorption, all without resorting to expensive or toxic elements.

Element‑by‑element rationale

Fe (carbon‑coordinated node, [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−)
- Catalytic activity: Although the hexacyanoferrate Fe is not typically the primary UOR active site, Fe3+/Fe2+ couples facilitate intervalence charge transfer through the CN bridge, lowering the barrier for Ni/Co oxidation to the catalytically active oxyhydroxide states. Fe also participates in synergistic electronic modulation of neighboring Ni/Co sites known to enhance UOR/OER kinetics.
- Structural stability: Fe(CN)6 is the most robust and ubiquitous hexacyanometallate; its strong-field, low‑spin octahedral geometry yields a rigid framework with high resistance to hydrolysis, anchoring the entire lattice. Fe(CN)6 vacancies can be tuned (y < 1) to introduce porosity and water sites without collapsing the network.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3[Fe(CN)6] is inexpensive, water‑soluble, and forms PBAs readily with a wide range of divalent cations at ambient conditions; co‑precipitation, sequential growth, or ligand‑exchange routes all work.
- Cost‑effectiveness and environmental aspects: Iron is abundant and cheap. The cyanide is strongly bound; in neutral/alkaline synthesis and use, PBAs do not release free CN−. Avoid acidic conditions in handling to maintain this safety margin.
- Structural features: Fe(C-bound) nodes provide strong M–C≡N–M′ bridges, high electronic delocalization, and a percolating network for electron transport during UOR.

Ni (nitrogen‑coordinated site)
- Catalytic activity: Ni is the principal UOR active center in alkaline media. It cycles between Ni2+ and Ni3+ (and Ni4+ under bias) to form NiOOH*, which mediates urea dehydrogenation and C–N bond cleavage. Ni provides low overpotentials for UOR relative to many first‑row metals.
- Stability: In a high‑entropy environment, Ni is less prone to over‑oxidation or dissolution; neighboring Fe/Co/Mn/Zn modulate Ni’s redox potential and OH− binding, maintaining activity over long cycling.
- Synthetic feasibility: Ni2+ readily occupies the N-coordinated site in PBAs. Mixed Ni/Co/ Mn/Zn salts co‑precipitate smoothly with hexacyanoferrate at room temperature.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Ni salts are widely available and relatively low‑cost, making Ni a practical choice for scale‑up.
- Structural features: Octahedral Ni–N coordination fits the PBA lattice well, and Ni’s d8 configuration produces suitable ligand‑field splitting for rapid electron hopping through M–N–C bridges.

Co (nitrogen‑coordinated site)
- Catalytic activity: Co augments Ni’s UOR kinetics by promoting OH− adsorption and facilitating the Ni2+→Ni3+ transition. Co centers can themselves become active (CoOOH*) and improve charge transfer, reducing the Tafel slope in UOR.
- Stability: Co in a multi‑cation environment resists phase segregation and maintains the mixed oxyhydroxide shell formed in situ, contributing to long‑term durability.
- Synthetic feasibility: Co2+ co‑precipitates cleanly with Ni2+ and Mn2+ in PBAs; its ionic radius and preferred octahedral geometry are compatible with the framework.
- Cost/environment: Co is more supply‑constrained than Fe/Mn/Zn but still far cheaper than noble metals. Using equimolar, modest fractions limits the total Co content. Avoid Co(III) hexacyanides to minimize synthesis complexity; stick to Co2+ on the N site.
- Structural features: Co’s d7 electronic structure tunes the local ligand field and introduces heterogeneous active ensembles next to Ni, improving the spectrum of adsorption energies for urea and intermediates.

Mn (nitrogen‑coordinated site)
- Catalytic activity: Mn provides multi‑valent redox flexibility (Mn2+/3+/4+), enhancing lattice oxygen participation and OH− affinity. It can facilitate the oxidative dehydrogenation steps and help break C–N bonds via modified M–OH and M–NH2 interactions.
- Stability: Mn introduces controlled Jahn–Teller distortions (particularly Mn3+) and local strain that increase defect density beneficial for catalysis, while high‑entropy mixing prevents large‑scale distortion or phase change. It also mitigates over‑oxidation of Ni/Co by sharing redox load.
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ readily integrates on the N site; mixed Ni/Co/Mn PBAs are well documented and synthesized in water without special conditions.
- Cost/environment: Mn is abundant, inexpensive, and environmentally benign compared with Cr/V.
- Structural features: The larger radius of Mn2+ relative to Ni2+/Co2+ introduces microstrain and enhances porosity when combined with Fe(CN)6 vacancies, improving mass transport of urea and OH−.

Zn (nitrogen‑coordinated site; the “stable, benign” addition)
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ (d10) is generally redox‑inactive under UOR, but its role is beneficial indirectly: it moderates the local electronic environment of Ni/Co/Fe, broadening the distribution of adsorption energies and smoothing reaction pathways. Zn can weaken overly strong OH− binding on Ni/Co sites, improving turnover.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: Zn2+ has a single stable valence and forms strong Zn–N bonds, acting as a structural anchor that suppresses cation leaching and stabilizes the framework under alkaline bias. It reduces the tendency of Ni/Co to dissolve and mitigates surface reconstruction extremes, extending catalyst life.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ mixes readily with other divalent cations in PBA synthesis, and its inclusion does not complicate precipitation kinetics or require special buffers.
- Cost/environment: Very low cost and non‑toxic. Introducing Zn satisfies the goal of adding a benign, stable‑valence element to optimize performance without sacrificing affordability.
- Structural features: Zn’s ionic size complements Ni/Co, helping achieve dense, but defect‑rich, microstructures. As a “spectator” cation, it raises configurational entropy and stabilizes the solid solution without introducing problematic redox behavior.

Synergy and high‑entropy benefits
- Configurational entropy: Equimolar mixing of Ni/Co/Mn/Zn on the N site yields a single‑phase solid solution, suppressing cation clustering and phase separation during cycling. This entropic stabilization reduces dissolution and enhances corrosion resistance in strong alkaline electrolytes.
- Electronic synergy: CN bridges mediate strong M–C–N coupling; Fe(CN)6 nodes enable rapid intervalence transfer. Ni provides primary UOR activity; Co and Mn tune OH− and intermediate binding; Zn stabilizes the landscape. The result is a broadened density of states and a distribution of adsorption energies that reduce kinetic barriers and enhance tolerance to urea concentration/pH fluctuations.
- Defect/vacancy engineering: Setting y < 1 introduces [Fe(CN)6] vacancies and interstitial water, increasing porosity and ionic transport. Mixed cation sizes (Mn2+ > Co2+ ≈ Zn2+ > Ni2+) generate microstrain that, combined with vacancies, creates more edge sites and accelerates charge/mass transfer.

Why avoid certain alternatives
- Noble metals (Ru, Ir, Pt) and high‑valence early transition metals (Mo, W) provide high activity but fail the cost and environmental criteria.
- Cr and V can form stable hexacyanometallates but pose environmental and regulatory concerns (Cr6+ toxicity, V leaching).
- Cu is often used to enhance conductivity and urea adsorption; however, replacing Cu with Zn improves corrosion resistance, lowers cost, and avoids potential Cu leaching in strong base, while retaining high activity via the dominant Ni/Co/Fe pathway.

Synthesis pathway (easy, scalable)
- Aqueous co‑precipitation at room temperature: dissolve Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ salts (e.g., nitrates/chlorides) in water; add K3[Fe(CN)6] under stirring. Use mild complexants (citric acid, EDTA) to control nucleation and achieve uniform multi‑cation incorporation. Adjust stoichiometry to set y (vacancy level) and n (water content).
- Growth on conductive supports (Ni foam, carbon cloth) yields binder‑free electrodes. Post‑synthetic aging/rinsing removes residual salts; low‑temperature drying preserves porosity. No high‑temperature calcination is required, minimizing energy and environmental footprint.
- Composition control: target equimolar N‑site cations for high entropy; verify by ICP‑OES/XPS. Fine‑tune y via Fe(CN)6:divalent cation ratio; vacancies typically 10–30% are beneficial for mass transport.

Performance/stability expectations in alkaline UOR
- The catalyst surface will reconstruct under anodic potentials to a mixed (Ni,Co,Fe,Mn,Zn) oxyhydroxide layer, the known active phase for UOR, while the PBA core maintains conductivity and mechanical support.
- High‑entropy mixing and Zn inclusion curb cation dissolution and microcracking, enhancing corrosion resistance and cycling stability.
- All selected metals are abundant and low‑cost; synthesis uses water and ambient conditions; environmental concerns are mitigated by avoiding toxic metals and by the strong cyanide binding in the PBA framework.

In sum, the Fe–(Ni,Co,Mn,Zn) high‑entropy PBA balances high UOR activity (Ni/Co‑driven, Fe‑assisted), structural stability (Fe(CN)6 framework, entropy stabilization), corrosion resistance (Zn anchoring, suppressed dissolution), cost‑effectiveness (first‑row metals only), and ease of synthesis (one‑pot aqueous co‑precipitation). The deliberate, environmentally benign inclusion of Zn2+ provides a stable‑valence, low‑toxicity element that optimizes durability without sacrificing catalytic performance.
