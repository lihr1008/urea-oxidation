Proposed catalyst: a high-entropy Prussian blue analog with the general formula A x(Ni, Mn, Cu, Zn)[Fe(CN)6]y·nH2O, where A is an alkali cation (K+/Na+/NH4+), the N-coordinated octahedral sites are randomly occupied by Ni2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, and Zn2+ in near-equimolar ratios, and the C-coordinated site is [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−. This yields five transition metals in total: Ni, Mn, Cu, Zn (N-site) and Fe (C-site). A modest [Fe(CN)6] vacancy fraction (y ≈ 0.85–0.95) is deliberately introduced to tune porosity and ion transport.

Why this architecture
- High configurational entropy on the N-site stabilizes the fcc PBA lattice against phase segregation and corrosion while creating a distribution of electronic environments that broaden the window of optimal binding energies for urea and intermediates.
- The [Fe(CN)6] building block is low-cost, robust, and synthetically straightforward; maintaining Fe exclusively on the C-site avoids the need for expensive or less benign hexacyanometallates (e.g., Ru/Ir/Co/Cr).
- The N-site blend is explicitly cobalt- and chromium-free to reduce cost, sourcing, and environmental burdens. A “benign, stable-valence” element (Zn2+) is intentionally introduced to enhance structural and electrochemical stability without sacrificing activity.

Element-by-element rationale

1) Nickel (Ni)
- Catalytic activity: Ni is the primary active center for urea oxidation in alkaline media. Under anodic polarization, surface Ni2+ converts to NiOOH, which activates urea via dehydrogenation and facilitates C–N bond cleavage and N–N coupling steps. Ni-based centers consistently show the lowest onset potentials and highest turnover for UOR among 3d metals.
- Stability: Ni(OH)2/NiOOH cycles reversibly in alkaline electrolytes, forming a robust oxyhydroxide shell that resists corrosion. Embedding Ni in a rigid PBA lattice suppresses Ni dissolution.
- Synthetic feasibility: Ni2+ readily integrates into the N-coordinated sites during simple room-temperature co-precipitation with K3[Fe(CN)6].
- Cost-effectiveness: Ni salts are inexpensive and widely available, offering excellent activity per dollar compared with noble metals.
- Structural features: Octahedral, N-coordinated Ni2+ interacts strongly through the cyanide bridge with [Fe(CN)6], adjusting the Ni2+/Ni3+ redox potential and facilitating in situ reconstruction to a conductive Ni-rich oxyhydroxide surface while maintaining a PBA core framework.

2) Iron (Fe)
- Catalytic activity: Fe is present mainly as [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− (C site). Through strong-field cyanide bridging, Fe tunes the ligand field around N-site metals, modulating their electron density and the potential at which Ni converts to NiOOH. This can sharpen the redox transition and increase the population of highly active γ-NiOOH-like species during UOR.
- Stability: Hexacyanoferrate frameworks are the most stable and well-studied PB/PBA motifs, with controlled vacancy chemistry and established electrochemical robustness in alkaline media.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3[Fe(CN)6]/K4[Fe(CN)6] are cheap, water-soluble, and enable clean, room-temperature, aqueous synthesis with predictable nucleation/growth into nanocubes, nanoframes, or thin films.
- Cost-effectiveness: Fe is among the least costly transition metals; using Fe on the C-site avoids the need for expensive or less benign hexacyanometallates (e.g., Ru/Ir/Co/Cr).
- Structural features: [Fe(CN)6] units define the fcc cyanide-bridged lattice, while a controlled fraction of [Fe(CN)6] vacancies introduces water-filled channels that improve OH− and urea transport and allow a surface oxyhydroxide shell to form without catastrophic lattice collapse.

3) Manganese (Mn)
- Catalytic activity: Mn2+/Mn3+ offers redox flexibility that can accelerate charge transfer to Ni active sites and create local electronic heterogeneity that improves adsorption/desorption of urea intermediates. Mn can lower the energy barrier for the Ni2+→NiOOH transformation and enhance nucleophilic attack by OH− on adsorbed urea.
- Stability: While Mn can be more labile than Ni/Fe, high-entropy mixing suppresses localized dissolution by distributing strain and charge across many cation species. A thin surface oxyhydroxide that develops under operation tends to confine any Mn mobility to the outermost layers.
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ incorporates easily via co-precipitation; it also helps nucleation of small crystallites, increasing electrochemically active surface area.
- Cost-effectiveness and environmental profile: Mn salts are low cost and broadly available; Mn is generally considered more benign than Co or Cr.
- Structural features: High-spin Mn2+ can mildly distort the octahedral sites, increasing defect density and water content at/near vacancies—beneficial for mass transport and creating additional edge/corner sites that are catalytically active.

4) Copper (Cu)
- Catalytic activity: Cu incorporation improves electronic conductivity of PBAs and can synergize with Ni to facilitate urea adsorption and lower activation barriers for C–N bond scission. Cu sites can also serve as auxiliary adsorption centers, broadening the distribution of binding energies for reaction intermediates.
- Stability: Although Cu can dissolve under strongly oxidizing conditions, embedding it in the PBA lattice and operating within the UOR potential window mitigates leaching. The formation of a mixed Ni–Fe–Mn–Cu oxyhydroxide surface further immobilizes Cu.
- Synthetic feasibility: Cu2+ salts co-precipitate cleanly with [Fe(CN)6] under mild, aqueous conditions, enabling scalable synthesis on porous substrates (Ni foam, carbon cloth).
- Cost-effectiveness: Cu is inexpensive and globally abundant.
- Structural features: Cu2+ (Jahn–Teller active) introduces controlled local distortions that increase the number of coordinatively unsaturated or defect-adjacent metal sites, often beneficial for catalytic turnover while the overall high-entropy matrix maintains long-range crystallinity.

5) Zinc (Zn) — the intentionally introduced “benign, stable-valence” element
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ (d10) is not a primary redox-active site for UOR, but it is valuable as an electronic and structural modulator. It dilutes overly strong adsorption sites, helping avoid site poisoning by intermediates and enabling faster product release. Zn can also subtly shift the d-band center of neighboring Ni/Cu sites through inductive effects across the CN bridge.
- Stability: Zn2+ has a stable valence and minimal redox participation in the UOR potential range, acting as a structural anchor that suppresses catastrophic lattice distortion. It also reduces the probability of deep over-oxidation of Ni sites by moderating local electron density.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ incorporates readily in the PBA lattice by simple co-precipitation at room temperature and does not require special handling.
- Cost-effectiveness and environmental profile: Zn is abundant, low-cost, and comparatively benign, providing a greener alternative to commonly used Co. This aligns with the aim to avoid higher-cost/concern metals.
- Structural features: Because Zn2+ is not Jahn–Teller active, it counterbalances the distortions introduced by Cu2+ and Mn2+, improving crystallinity and mechanical integrity while maintaining high defect density and ion transport pathways.

Why avoid other common metals
- Co: Strong UOR/OER promoter but higher cost, supply-chain and ethical sourcing concerns, and increased environmental scrutiny. The Ni–Mn–Cu–Zn blend with Fe(CN)6 preserves activity while lowering risk and cost.
- Cr: Although [Cr(CN)6]3− is known, chromium carries environmental toxicity concerns (hexavalent species risks), making it less desirable for green catalysis.
- Noble metals (Ru, Ir, Pt): Excellent activity but prohibitively expensive and less sustainable for large-scale wastewater-to-hydrogen applications.
- Mo/W/V/Ti cyanide building blocks: Less common or unstable in aqueous cyanide frameworks, complicating synthesis and increasing cost/complexity.

Structural and compositional tuning recommendations
- N-site ratios: Near-equimolar Ni/Mn/Cu/Zn to maximize configurational entropy; optionally bias Ni slightly (for example, Ni0.35Mn0.20Cu0.20Zn0.25) to maximize active NiOOH while retaining entropy-driven stability.
- Vacancy fraction: Target y ≈ 0.9 for a balanced trade-off between mechanical stability and accessible porosity/ion channels. Vacancies host water/hydroxide and promote OH−/urea transport.
- Alkali cation (A): K+ often yields well-faceted nanocubes with good ionic conductivity; Na+ or NH4+ can be used for cost or growth control.
- Morphology: Nanocubes, mesoporous aggregates, or hollow nanoframes (via mild etching) increase surface area while the rigid PBA scaffold resists collapse.
- Operando surface reconstruction: Deliberately pre-cycle the catalyst in alkaline electrolyte containing urea to form a thin, conformal Ni-rich mixed oxyhydroxide shell on a PBA core. This core–shell arrangement enhances activity and confers corrosion resistance by passivating the outer surface while maintaining electronic connectivity to the robust core.

Synthesis pathway (scalable, green, low-cost)
- Aqueous, room-temperature co-precipitation: Mix an aqueous solution of Ni2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, Zn2+ salts (chlorides or nitrates) with K3[Fe(CN)6] under mild stirring; control pH to avoid hydroxide precipitation (use slight acidity or citrate as a complexing agent), then age, wash, and dry. Grow directly on conductive supports (Ni foam, carbon cloth) to reduce contact resistance and binder use.
- Optional steps: Incorporate a benign structure-directing agent (PVP) for shape control; introduce a small fraction of vacancies by stoichiometry; perform mild alkaline activation cycles.
- Environmental aspects: Water as solvent, ambient temperature, no high-temperature calcination, no toxic oxidants. Cyanide is immobilized in the lattice; standard aqueous handling and waste protocols suffice.

Expected performance characteristics and durability factors
- Activity: Ni-centered sites activated by Fe-bridging and modulated by Mn/Cu electronic effects afford low UOR onset potentials and high current density at modest overpotentials, typically outperforming single- or binary-metal PBAs.
- Stability/corrosion resistance: High-entropy mixing distributes mechanical and electronic strain, suppressing dissolution of more labile cations; Zn2+ stabilizes the lattice; an in situ mixed oxyhydroxide skin protects against harsh alkaline conditions and carbonates formed during UOR.
- Cost and sustainability: All five metals are earth-abundant and relatively low cost; the synthesis is energy-efficient and scalable; the formulation is cobalt- and chromium-free.

In summary, the Ni–Mn–Cu–Zn//Fe high-entropy PBA satisfies the multi-objective requirements for alkaline urea oxidation: Ni provides the primary active function; Fe(CN)6 supplies a robust, low-cost framework and tunes redox thermodynamics; Mn and Cu enhance electronic conductivity, redox kinetics, and active-site diversity; Zn is the intentionally introduced, environmentally benign stabilizer that boosts structural integrity and long-term corrosion resistance without significant cost or complexity. This combination leverages entropy-driven stability and multi-site synergy to deliver high catalytic activity, durability, facile synthesis, and a favorable environmental and economic profile.
