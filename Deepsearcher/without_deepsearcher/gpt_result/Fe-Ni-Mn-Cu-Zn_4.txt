Proposed catalyst: a high-entropy Prussian blue analog with a mixed A-site of four abundant divalent metals (Ni2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, Zn2+) coordinated to the N end of cyanide and a B-site of Fe3+ in [FeIII(CN)6]3−. A representative formula is (Ni, Mn, Cu, Zn)3[Fe(CN)6]2·zH2O with deliberate [Fe(CN)6] vacancies and lattice water to maximize surface area and mass transport. This five-transition-metal selection (Ni, Fe, Cu, Mn, Zn) balances urea oxidation activity, structural stability, cost, and environmental considerations while avoiding higher-cost or more problematic elements such as Co, Cr, V, and noble metals.

Why this metal combination works for UOR in alkaline media

- High-entropy effect:
  - Mixing four different A-site metals in near-equimolar ratios produces lattice distortion, chemical disorder, and “cocktail” effects that slow cation migration and dissolution in alkaline electrolyte. This improves corrosion resistance and structural integrity during long-term operation and under repeated redox cycling.
  - Local compositional heterogeneity tunes adsorption energetics for urea and intermediates across a spectrum of active sites, raising the probability of having optimal local environments without relying on a single metal.

- PBA framework advantages:
  - The robust Fe–C≡N–M connectivity provides fast electron transport pathways and a stable, open framework with micro/mesopores that facilitate urea diffusion and product evacuation (notably carbonate in alkaline media).
  - Cyanide is strongly bound to Fe3+, and the Fe–C bond is particularly stable; in alkaline electrolytes the hexacyanoferrate framework remains intact, while the surface can reconstruct to form catalytically active (oxy)hydroxides.
  - Vacancies in [Fe(CN)6] units and interstitial water increase accessible active sites and moderate lattice strain.

Element-by-element rationale

1) Ni (primary UOR-active component)
- Catalytic activity: Ni is the most established non-noble UOR center in alkaline media. Under anodic bias, surface Ni2+ converts to Ni3+ (β-/γ-NiOOH), which is widely recognized as the active phase for urea dehydrogenation. Ni sites effectively bind and activate urea, facilitating successive dehydrogenation steps at lower potentials than oxygen evolution.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: In alkaline solution, Ni forms stable hydroxide/oxyhydroxide layers that are self-passivating yet catalytically active; embedding Ni in the PBA reduces sintering and dissolution and buffers volume changes upon redox cycling.
- Synthetic feasibility: Ni2+ salts readily form Ni–Fe PBAs at room temperature via simple co-precipitation with K3[Fe(CN)6]. Ni integrates well with Mn, Cu, and Zn in mixed A-sites without significant phase segregation when moderate citrate or pH control is used.
- Cost/environment: Ni is abundant and relatively low cost, with mature supply chains and manageable EHS profile in common salts.
- Structural features: Ni2+ (high-spin d8 in octahedral N coordination) offers favorable eg occupancy for forming active NiOOH. In the PBA, Ni–N–C–Fe bridges provide strong coupling, while Ni-rich local environments at the surface under operation deliver high UOR turnover.

2) Fe (framework former and electronic synergist)
- Catalytic activity: While Fe is not the primary UOR active site in alkaline media, Fe strongly modulates Ni’s redox and electronic structure. In Ni–Fe systems, Fe incorporation lowers the Ni2+/Ni3+ redox potential and improves charge transfer, which typically translates to higher intrinsic activity and lower onset potentials for UOR.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: Using [FeIII(CN)6]3− as the B-site builds a mechanically and chemically robust backbone. Fe–C bonds resist hydrolysis; the cyanide ligand remains tightly bound in alkaline conditions, minimizing ligand loss and maintaining framework integrity during cycling.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3[Fe(CN)6] is a standard, low-cost precursor for PBAs; room-temperature precipitation yields uniform particles or direct growth on conductive substrates (e.g., Ni foam).
- Cost/environment: Iron is inexpensive, earth-abundant, and benign compared to many transition metals. Hexacyanoferrate solids are considered safe under neutral/alkaline conditions (avoid strong acids to prevent HCN release).
- Structural features: Fe is most stably fixed in the B-site as [Fe(CN)6]3−, supporting high crystallinity and conduction pathways. It also enables controlled [Fe(CN)6] vacancy creation (defect engineering) to increase accessible sites and tune hydrophilicity.

3) Cu (conductivity enhancer and adsorption-energy tuner)
- Catalytic activity: Cu2+/3+ centers can improve the kinetics of electron transfer in mixed-metal hydroxides and alter the adsorption energetics of urea and intermediates. In Ni–Cu systems, Cu often increases electrical conductivity and can lower activation barriers for dehydrogenation steps.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: In alkaline media, bulk Cu can leach under oxidative potentials, but within the PBA, strong Cu–N–C–Fe coordination retards dissolution. High-entropy mixing further suppresses Cu segregation and leaching by distributing Cu at isolated lattice positions.
- Synthetic feasibility: Cu2+ integrates easily into PBAs by co-precipitation. Potential Jahn–Teller distortion (d9 Cu2+) is mitigated by the presence of Zn and Mn and by the inherent flexibility of the hydrated PBA lattice.
- Cost/environment: Copper is relatively low cost and widely available; EHS concerns are manageable in solid frameworks and with standard waste handling.
- Structural features: Cu contributes to enhanced electrical pathways through the lattice and, through local distortion, broadens the distribution of M–N bond lengths, which helps tune catalytic site geometries.

4) Mn (redox flexibility and lattice stabilization)
- Catalytic activity: Mn can cycle between multiple oxidation states (Mn2+/3+/4+), influencing adjacent Ni sites by modifying local electron density and facilitating lattice oxygen participation when beneficial. In UOR, Mn often plays a secondary role that tunes the NiOOH redox behavior and can improve intermediate desorption.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: Mn’s presence stabilizes the framework by accommodating strain during redox transitions and distributing local distortions arising from Cu2+. As part of a high-entropy mix, Mn helps suppress long-range ordering that could lead to crack formation upon cycling.
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ salts readily co-precipitate; by adjusting pH and using mild complexants (e.g., citrate), homogeneous Mn incorporation is straightforward at room temperature.
- Cost/environment: Manganese is abundant and low-cost with a favorable environmental profile.
- Structural features: High-spin Mn2+ increases lattice flexibility and can reduce the Jahn–Teller impact of Cu while also promoting defect tolerance. It helps maintain framework integrity during surface reconstruction to (oxy)hydroxides.

5) Zn (environmentally benign structural promoter and corrosion mitigator)
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ is largely redox-inactive under UOR conditions, acting primarily as a structural diluent that “site isolates” highly active (and sometimes overly reactive) Ni sites, moderating adsorption strength to prevent over-binding of intermediates.
- Stability and corrosion resistance: Zn strongly stabilizes the PBA lattice by reducing the incidence of cooperative distortions and by increasing crystallinity and surface area. It also improves resistance to cation leaching and mitigates micro-galvanic corrosion pathways by diluting more reactive metals.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ incorporates easily with minimal tendency to form secondary phases; its stable +2 state and ionic radius are compatible with the A-site.
- Cost/environment: Zinc is inexpensive, abundant, and environmentally benign, aligning with the requirement to introduce a stable-valence, green element. It is a preferred alternative to cobalt in this role when cost/EHS are prioritized.
- Structural features: Zn suppresses long-range order and promotes high surface area nanostructures. It tempers Cu-related Jahn–Teller distortions and supports defect generation (vacancies) without catastrophic framework collapse.

Why we deliberately avoid certain metals
- Co: Highly effective for alkaline electrocatalysis but costs more than Fe/Mn/Zn and carries greater supply-chain and ethical concerns. The proposed Ni–Fe–Cu–Mn–Zn mix maintains high activity while reducing Co dependence.
- Cr and V: Potential toxicity and environmental concerns, and limited added value for UOR relative to the chosen set.
- Noble metals (Ru, Ir, Pt): Excellent activity for related reactions but incompatible with low-cost, scalable, and environmentally friendly criteria.
- Mo/W: Can enhance UOR in some systems but add cost and complexity; their benefits can be approximated here via Ni–Fe synergy and Cu/Mn effects.

Structure–function relationships under operating conditions
- Surface reconstruction: Under UOR potentials in alkaline media, the outer PBA layers reconstruct into mixed (Ni,Fe,Cu,Mn) oxyhydroxides with NiOOH as the dominant active phase; the PBA core remains as a conductive, mechanically supportive scaffold. This core–shell configuration combines high activity with structural durability.
- Electronic synergy: Fe in [Fe(CN)6] and nearby Fe in oxyhydroxides lower the Ni redox threshold, while Cu improves lattice conductivity. Mn finetunes the redox window and strain tolerance; Zn maintains structural coherence and high surface area.
- Defects and water: [Fe(CN)6] vacancies, lattice water, and high-entropy disorder enlarge transport channels for urea and hydroxide, increase the number of accessible edge/corner sites, and promote fast ion/electron transport.

Synthesis approach (simple, scalable, and green)
- One-pot, aqueous co-precipitation at room temperature:
  - Dissolve Ni2+, Cu2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ salts (e.g., nitrates/sulfates/chlorides) in water with a mild complexant (citrate or EDTA) to slow nucleation and enable homogeneous mixing.
  - Add K3[Fe(CN)6] slowly under stirring; maintain mildly alkaline pH (≈7–9) to avoid acid conditions and to control particle growth.
  - Age at room temperature, collect by filtration, rinse thoroughly, and dry at low temperature.
  - Optional: grow directly on Ni foam or carbon cloth by immersing the substrate in the precursor solution for binder-free electrodes.
- Post-treatments: mild oxidative activation (e.g., cyclic voltammetry in KOH + urea) to form the active oxyhydroxide-rich surface; gentle annealing (≤150–200°C) can improve crystallinity without collapsing the hydrated framework.
- Environmental/safety notes: Hexacyanoferrate is safe to handle in neutral/alkaline media; avoid acidic conditions to prevent HCN liberation. Waste streams should be kept alkaline and treated appropriately.

Cost and sustainability
- All selected metals are abundant and inexpensive; precursors are commodity chemicals.
- The synthesis runs at room temperature in water without organic solvents, high pressure, or vacuum equipment.
- The design excludes Co, Cr, and noble metals, lowering cost and mitigating environmental concerns.

Corrosion resistance and durability in alkaline UOR
- High-entropy mixing and Zn inclusion suppress selective dissolution and micro-galvanic corrosion.
- The PBA’s robust Fe–C≡N–M framework endures alkaline conditions, while only the surface converts to (oxy)hydroxides—maintaining overall integrity.
- The UOR occurs at lower potentials than OER, reducing oxidative stress and limiting oxygen bubble damage to the electrode.

Composition guidance
- A-site target ratios (atomic): Ni 0.35–0.45, Mn 0.15–0.25, Cu 0.15–0.20, Zn 0.15–0.25; B-site: Fe as [Fe(CN)6]3− with 10–25% controlled vacancies for porosity. Ni-rich compositions prioritize UOR activity; Mn and Zn improve robustness; Cu optimizes conductivity and kinetics.
- Support: growth on conductive, corrosion-resistant substrates (Ni foam, Ni mesh, or carbon cloth) further decreases ohmic losses and enhances stability.

In summary, the Ni–Fe–Cu–Mn–Zn high-entropy PBA exploits Ni-driven UOR activity, Fe-based framework stability and Ni–Fe synergy, Cu-enabled conductivity and kinetic tuning, Mn’s redox/strain accommodation, and Zn’s benign structural stabilization. It achieves high activity and durability in alkaline media while meeting practical constraints of cost, synthesis simplicity, environmental friendliness, and corrosion resistance—without relying on cobalt or other metals with greater cost or EHS concerns.
