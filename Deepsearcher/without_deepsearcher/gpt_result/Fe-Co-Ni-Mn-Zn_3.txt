Proposed catalyst: a high‑entropy Prussian blue analog with formula Ax(Ni, Co, Fe, Mn, Zn)y[Fe(CN)6]·zH2O, where five transition metals occupy the N‑coordinated site (Ni, Co, Fe, Mn, Zn) and hexacyanoferrate [Fe(CN)6]n− serves as the C‑coordinated framework. The tentative “environmentally benign, stable‑valence” element introduced to optimize performance and stability is Zn2+.

Why a high‑entropy Prussian blue analog for alkaline UOR:
- Cyanide‑bridged frameworks provide a robust, electrically conductive network (through Fe–C≡N–M’ linkages), high surface area, abundant defects/vacancies, and facile ion/water transport channels. These features favor multi‑electron, multi‑step reactions like the urea oxidation reaction in alkaline environments.
- High‑entropy mixing (five metals) increases configurational entropy, suppresses phase segregation/sintering, creates a broad distribution of local electronic environments, and generates more coordinatively unsaturated sites and defects, which together enhance catalytic activity and cycling durability.
- PBAs are readily synthesized at room temperature from low‑cost salts (e.g., K3[Fe(CN)6] and metal nitrates/chlorides), meeting cost, scalability, and environmental compatibility goals.

Metal‑by‑metal rationale and contributions:

1) Ni (primary UOR activity driver)
- Catalytic activity: Ni is a benchmark for UOR in alkaline media because Ni2+ transforms in situ to Ni(III/IV) oxyhydroxide (β/γ‑NiOOH) under anodic bias, which is highly active for the dehydrogenation and C–N bond activation steps in urea. Ni–Fe synergy is well‑documented for alkaline anodic reactions; Fe adjacent to Ni lowers the Ni2+/Ni3+ redox potential and improves charge transfer, boosting intrinsic activity.
- Stability: In a PBA, Ni2+ sits in octahedral N‑coordination, strongly bound through Fe–C≡N–Ni linkages. Under operation, a thin oxyhydroxide shell forms while the PBA core stabilizes the particle, limiting dissolution and morphological collapse.
- Synthetic feasibility: Ni salts are ubiquitous and co‑precipitate cleanly with ferrocyanide. Ni2+ ionic radius and CN‑ligand field are compatible with forming uniform PBA nanocrystals.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Abundant, low‑cost, widely used.
- Structural features: Ni2+ in an N‑ligated octahedron tunes its eg electron occupancy close to unity when coupled with Fe(CN)6, favoring redox kinetics. Local Ni–Co–Fe neighborhoods modulate the d‑band, improving urea adsorption and OH− activation.

2) Co (redox promoter and conductivity enhancer)
- Catalytic activity: Co participates in Co2+/Co3+ redox and forms CoOOH under alkaline bias. As a neighbor to Ni, Co broadens the redox window and accelerates electron transfer, improving the kinetics of urea dehydrogenation and intermediates’ turnover (e.g., *CONH2, *NHx, *CO).
- Stability: Co in the PBA is immobilized via strong cyanide bridges; high‑entropy mixing reduces Co leaching by dispersing Co in a multi‑metal environment where local reconstruction yields mixed Ni/Co/Fe oxyhydroxides rather than isolated Co phases.
- Synthetic feasibility: Co2+ co‑precipitates readily with ferrocyanide and integrates well with Ni/Fe/Mn/Zn in a single‑step synthesis.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Moderately priced. Using Co at a moderate fraction balances performance gains with cost and environmental considerations.
- Structural features: Co2+ substitution introduces microstrain and heterogeneity that increase defect density and active sites. It also enhances electronic connectivity through stronger metal–ligand covalency compared to purely Ni/Fe systems.

3) Fe (framework former and electronic promoter)
- Catalytic activity: Fe is present both as the hexacyanoferrate linker ([Fe(CN)6]3−/4−) and partially at N‑coordinated sites. Fe adjacent to Ni is known to improve alkaline anodic activity by acting as an electronic promoter and facilitating charge transfer, enhancing NiOOH’s reactivity toward urea.
- Stability: The Fe–C≡N scaffold is the structural backbone of PBAs; hexacyanoferrates are especially stable and environmentally benign compared to other cyanide complexes. Mixed‑valence Fe2+/Fe3+ enables electron‑hopping conductivity, which supports sustained redox cycling.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3[Fe(CN)6] is inexpensive, widely available, and forms PBAs under mild, aqueous conditions. Incorporating small fractions of Fe2+ on the N‑site is straightforward using ferrous salts.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Very low cost and earth‑abundant.
- Structural features: Fe(CN)6 octahedra establish rigid, open lattices with interstitial water and alkali cations (K+, Na+) that create ion/water transport channels, beneficial for mass transport and buffering local pH during UOR.

4) Mn (defect generator and redox buffer)
- Catalytic activity: Mn’s multiple accessible valences (Mn2+/3+/4+) modulate local electron density and can create oxygen vacancy‑like defects after surface activation, aiding the adsorption/activation of OH− and urea intermediates. In mixed Ni/Co/Fe environments, Mn can slightly weaken overly strong adsorption sites, improving turnover.
- Stability: Mn acts as a redox buffer that distributes oxidative load during potential cycling, mitigating over‑oxidation of Ni/Co sites. Its presence improves structural resilience by accommodating microstrain and preventing catastrophic local phase transitions.
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ salts co‑precipitate well; Mn2+ is compatible with the N‑coordinated site in PBAs.
- Cost‑effectiveness: Very low cost, highly abundant.
- Structural features: Mn2+ has a slightly different ionic radius than Ni/Co, intentionally introducing lattice microstrain and heterogeneous local environments. This increases defect density and active‑site diversity while preserving overall framework integrity in a high‑entropy lattice.

5) Zn (environmentally benign stabilizer with a stable valence)
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ is not a primary redox‑active UOR site (d10), but its role in a high‑entropy PBA is to optimize the catalyst’s stability, corrosion resistance, and microstructure. It moderates the local electronic environment, preventing excessive oxidation of Ni/Co under prolonged operation and can tune the adsorption strength of intermediates by diluting overly active sites, which helps sustain high turnover at practical current densities.
- Stability: Zn2+ has a stable valence and strong preference for octahedral N‑coordination in PBAs, which strengthens the framework and reduces metal dissolution in alkaline anolytes. Zn also helps control crystal growth and suppresses Ostwald ripening, improving durability.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ integrates cleanly in one‑pot co‑precipitation, enabling uniform multi‑metal distribution without requiring harsh conditions or specialized precursors.
- Cost‑effectiveness and environmental friendliness: Very low cost, abundant, and low toxicity. Zn is preferred over metals like Cr (environmental concerns, especially in hexavalent forms) or Cu (higher corrosion/dissolution risk under anodic alkaline conditions).
- Structural features: Zn introduces electronic heterogeneity without adding competing redox peaks. In a five‑metal lattice, Zn increases configurational entropy and defect diversity while preserving framework rigidity, supporting long‑term cycling.

Design notes: avoiding higher‑cost or environmentally concerning metals
- Noble metals (Ru, Ir, Pt, Pd, Rh, Os) are avoided due to high cost and scarcity. While highly active for OER, they are unnecessary for UOR when Ni‑based systems are optimized.
- Cr is avoided due to environmental and health concerns associated with hexavalent species; maintaining strictly trivalent Cr in synthesis and operation is nontrivial.
- Cu can enhance conductivity but often suffers from dissolution and corrosion in alkaline anodic environments; replacing Cu with Zn provides better durability and environmental compatibility.
- Mo/W/V/Ti cyanide chemistry is less common or synthetically challenging in PBA frameworks; incorporating them risks complexity, cost, and reduced reproducibility.

Suggested compositional balance and structural features
- N‑site atomic fractions (example): Ni 30–35%, Co 15–20%, Fe 10–20%, Mn 10–15%, Zn 10–15%. This maintains Ni as the dominant active site, uses Co as a strong promoter, keeps Fe/Mn as electronic/defect modulators, and employs Zn for stability and corrosion resistance. Minor tuning can be done based on desired current density and durability targets.
- Vacancies and interstitial cations: Maintain a modest vacancy level (substoichiometric [Fe(CN)6]) and intercalated K+ or Na+ to create ion channels and balance charge. Vacancies raise surface area and expose more M–N sites.
- Morphology: Target nanocubes or hollow nanospheres/nanoframes via controlled precipitation and etching; hollow structures improve mass transport, ECSA, and reduce diffusion limitations for urea and OH−.
- Operando reconstruction: Expect formation of a thin mixed Ni/Co/Fe oxyhydroxide shell under bias, with Mn/Zn largely remaining as lattice stabilizers. The PBA core acts as a conductive skeleton, limiting agglomeration and dissolution.

Synthesis approach (simple, scalable, and green)
- One‑pot aqueous co‑precipitation at room temperature: Slowly add K3[Fe(CN)6] to a mixed solution of Ni, Co, Fe, Mn, and Zn salts (e.g., nitrates), possibly with a mild chelating agent (citrate) to control nucleation. Adjust pH near neutral to mildly basic. Age for uniform growth, wash thoroughly, and dry at low temperature.
- Post‑treatment: Mild thermal dehydration to adjust zH2O, then electrochemical pre‑activation in 1 M KOH + 0.33 M urea to form the active oxyhydroxide shell. No harsh solvents or high temperatures needed.
- Supports/coatings: Deposit on nickel foam, carbon cloth, or stainless steel mesh for low resistance and mechanical robustness. Optional thin carbon coating can improve conductivity without compromising environmental goals.

Cost, environmental friendliness, and corrosion resistance
- All chosen metals are abundant and low‑cost; reagents are commodity chemicals. The synthesis is water‑based and room‑temperature, minimizing energy and chemical footprints.
- Ferrocyanide complexes are far more stable and safer than free cyanide; routine handling and waste protocols suffice to mitigate hazards.
- Zn inclusion improves corrosion resistance and long‑term cycling stability by moderating redox extremes and mitigating Ni/Co dissolution. Mn further buffers redox and distributes oxidative stress.
- Avoiding Co overload balances environmental considerations; using Co at moderate fractions preserves activity gains while minimizing potential environmental impact.

Expected performance profile in alkaline UOR
- High intrinsic activity from Ni‑centered redox with Co/Fe promotion, aided by large ECSA from defect‑rich, high‑entropy PBA nanostructures.
- Low onset potential and high current density due to improved conductivity (Fe‑mediated electron hopping) and optimized adsorption energetics (Ni–Co–Fe synergy).
- Strong durability under continuous operation: entropic stabilization, Zn/Mn‑enabled corrosion resistance, and PBA core skeleton that resists sintering and collapse during oxyhydroxide formation.

Summary recommendation
- Use a five‑metal high‑entropy PBA where Ni, Co, Fe, Mn, and Zn occupy the N‑coordinated metal sites, with [Fe(CN)6] providing the C‑coordinated framework. Keep Ni as the majority N‑site component, Co as a significant promoter, and introduce Zn as the tentatively selected, environmentally benign, stable‑valence stabilizer. This combination provides a balanced solution across catalytic activity, stability, synthesis simplicity, cost, environmental compatibility, and corrosion resistance for alkaline urea oxidation.
