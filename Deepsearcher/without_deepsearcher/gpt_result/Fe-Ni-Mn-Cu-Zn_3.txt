Design summary
- Target composition (five transition metals total): (Ni0.25Cu0.25Mn0.25Zn0.25)–Fe Prussian blue analog, i.e., an equimolar quaternary mix of Ni, Cu, Mn, and Zn on the N-coordinated A-site bridged to [Fe(CN)6] on the C-coordinated B′-site, with a controlled fraction of [Fe(CN)6] vacancies and interstitial water. This keeps the total number of metals at five (Ni, Cu, Mn, Zn, Fe).
- Rationale: Ni provides the primary urea oxidation sites; Fe in the [Fe(CN)6] framework supplies electronic mediation and the archetypal PBA structure; Cu promotes urea adsorption and C–N cleavage while enhancing conductivity; Mn tunes redox and creates beneficial lattice disorder; Zn is deliberately introduced as the environmentally benign, stable-valence element to improve structural stability, mitigate Jahn–Teller distortions from Cu2+, and reduce cost while maintaining high-entropy stabilization.
- Avoided metals: precious (Ru, Ir, Pt, Pd), toxicity-prone (Cr, Cd, Pb, Hg), and supply-risk/cost-sensitive choices (Co, to the extent possible). Co often boosts activity but is omitted here to prioritize sustainability and cost while introducing Zn2+ to retain entropy and stability.

Why high-entropy PBA for alkaline UOR
- The cyanide-bridged cubic framework A–N≡C–B′ produces 3D ion/water channels, facilitating OH−/CO3 2− transport and exposure of redox sites.
- Multi-metal (high-entropy) occupancy on the A-site increases the configurational entropy, stabilizing a single phase against segregation under electrochemical conditions.
- Local lattice distortion and a moderate density of [Fe(CN)6] vacancies create additional accessible sites and shorten diffusion paths, while the Fe2+/Fe3+ couple provides intervalence charge transfer that improves electronic conductivity relative to single-metal PBAs.

Element-by-element rationale

1) Ni (primary UOR-active center)
- Catalytic activity: In alkaline media, Ni2+ is readily oxidized to γ-NiOOH/Ni3+ (and locally Ni4+) under anodic bias; these species are widely regarded as the most active for UOR, lowering the onset potential relative to other first-row metals. Ni–O sites are effective for nucleophilic attack on urea and for breaking C–N bonds at modest potentials.
- Stability: Within the PBA, Ni is strongly N-bound, and during operation the surface reconstructs to a thin Ni(Fe)-oxyhydroxide layer while the cyanide framework underneath helps maintain morphology—this “core–shell” behavior often improves durability in alkaline UOR, which operates at lower potentials than OER.
- Synthetic feasibility: Ni2+ incorporates readily into PBAs via room-temperature co-precipitation with K3[Fe(CN)6], producing well-defined nanocubes or nanoframes.
- Cost-effectiveness: Ni is abundant and low cost for transition-metal catalysis.
- Structural features: Ni2+ (octahedral) is not strongly Jahn–Teller active, offering a structurally robust A-site occupant. Its presence also tunes the Fe2+/Fe3+ intervalence conductivity through electronic coupling along the Ni–N≡C–Fe chain.

2) Fe (framework and electronic mediator as [Fe(CN)6])
- Catalytic activity: While Fe in the B′-site is not the primary UOR center, Fe3+/Fe2+ couples facilitate electron transport (intervalence charge transfer). Surface reconstruction yields Ni–Fe (oxy)hydroxide motifs that synergistically lower the Ni redox overpotential and accelerate UOR kinetics.
- Stability: Hexacyanoferrate forms the archetypal PBA framework with strong M–N≡C–Fe bonds, conferring robust crystallinity and porosity. The Fe(CN)6 network also stabilizes mixed A-site occupancies.
- Synthetic feasibility: K3[Fe(CN)6] is water-soluble, inexpensive, and yields PBAs at room temperature with controllable defect levels.
- Cost-effectiveness and environmental impact: Fe is among the cheapest and most benign transition metals; ferricyanide is stable and, when handled at neutral/alkaline pH, poses low risk of free cyanide release.
- Structural features: Fe on the B′-site defines the cubic framework and, through its redox flexibility, enhances overall electronic conductivity of the PBA—a known limitation of cyanide frameworks addressed here without adding expensive conductors.

3) Cu (urea adsorption promoter and conductivity enhancer)
- Catalytic activity: Cu sites coordinate urea strongly, lowering the barrier for C–N bond cleavage and facilitating the formation of key intermediates. In multimetal systems, Cu improves pre-adsorption of urea near Ni active centers, increasing the local reaction rate.
- Stability: Cu2+ is Jahn–Teller active (d9), which can introduce local distortions and defects; moderate Cu content in a high-entropy matrix spreads this strain, avoiding framework collapse while benefiting from defect-induced activity.
- Synthetic feasibility: Cu2+ readily co-precipitates into PBAs. Using a chelating agent (e.g., citrate) during synthesis evens out incorporation kinetics versus Ni/Mn/Zn for compositional homogeneity.
- Cost-effectiveness: Cu is abundant, low cost, and easily sourced.
- Structural features: Controlled Jahn–Teller distortion opens channels and creates edge/corner sites, improving mass transport and exposure of active sites. Cu also contributes to electronic conduction through enhanced mixed-valence pathways along Cu–N≡C–Fe linkages.

4) Mn (redox tuner and defect/strain engineering)
- Catalytic activity: Mn can access Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+ redox states; in a high-entropy PBA it acts as a redox buffer that fine-tunes the potential at which Ni oxidizes to NiOOH. It can also facilitate lattice oxygen participation and influence adsorption energetics of intermediates.
- Stability: Distributed Mn across the A-site increases the energy barrier for cation migration (entropy stabilization), and controlled Mn content avoids excessive dissolution that can occur at higher potentials (noting UOR operates below harsh OER potentials).
- Synthetic feasibility: Mn2+ salts co-precipitate cleanly into PBAs at room temperature; using mild complexants helps maintain uniform distribution.
- Cost-effectiveness: Mn is inexpensive and widely available.
- Structural features: Mn introduces microstrain and local disorder beneficial for catalysis. It can also compensate charge for CN vacancies, enabling vacancy engineering without destabilizing the lattice.

5) Zn (environmentally benign, stable-valence stabilizer)
- Catalytic activity: Zn2+ is largely redox-inactive under UOR conditions; it serves as a structural “diluent” that prevents overconcentration of highly distorted Cu2+ sites and overly active Ni sites from clustering, which can trigger local dissolution. Its presence maintains high entropy while tempering surface reconstruction, indirectly sustaining turnover by preserving accessible Ni sites.
- Stability: Zn2+ (d10) has a stable octahedral coordination and is non–Jahn–Teller active, counterbalancing Cu’s distortions and improving long-term structural integrity and corrosion resistance. Strong N coordination in the PBA mitigates Zn leaching in alkaline UOR windows.
- Synthetic feasibility: Zn2+ integrates easily into PBAs at room temperature with aqueous reagents.
- Cost-effectiveness and environmental impact: Zn is abundant, low cost, and considered environmentally benign—making it an ideal “introduced” stabilizer to replace more problematic elements like Co or Cr while preserving high-entropy benefits.
- Structural features: Zn smooths the distribution of local bond lengths and maintains a single-phase solid solution, reducing internal stress concentrations and helping sustain crystallinity through cycling.

Why not include Co (and other exclusions)
- Co is a strong activity promoter in many alkaline oxidations, but it is costlier, has supply-chain and toxicity concerns relative to Fe/Ni/Mn/Zn/Cu, and can complicate end-of-life management. Cr and V raise toxicity concerns (CrVI, V species). Precious metals violate the low-cost criterion. Replacing Co with Zn preserves entropy and improves eco-profile while retaining performance via Ni–Fe–Cu–Mn synergy.

Structural and materials design choices
- Framework: A-site equimolar Ni/Cu/Mn/Zn with B′-site Fe to maximize entropy while leveraging the well-known Fe-hexacyanide backbone. Target a moderate [Fe(CN)6] vacancy fraction (e.g., 10–25%) to enhance porosity and water/OH−/urea transport without sacrificing mechanical stability.
- Morphology: Nanocubes or nanoframes (50–200 nm) for high surface area and short diffusion lengths. A thin, in situ–formed Ni(Fe)-oxyhydroxide skin under operation provides the catalytic surface while the PBA core maintains structural integrity.
- Conductivity: Rely on Fe intervalence charge transfer and Cu incorporation for improved conduction; for practical electrodes, grow the HE-PBA directly on conductive supports (e.g., Ni foam, carbon cloth) to minimize ohmic losses while keeping synthesis simple and binder-free.

Synthesis route (easy, green, scalable)
- Aqueous room-temperature co-precipitation:
  - Prepare an equimolar solution of Ni2+, Cu2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ (nitrates or sulfates) with a mild chelator (e.g., sodium citrate) to harmonize incorporation kinetics.
  - Add K3[Fe(CN)6] slowly under vigorous stirring at near-neutral to mildly alkaline pH (avoid strong acid to prevent any cyanide release; operate in closed vessels and maintain alkaline waste streams).
  - Control [Fe(CN)6] stoichiometry to tune vacancy density; age, wash, and dry. Optionally, seed or grow in situ on Ni foam or carbon cloth for direct electrodes.
- Environmental notes: Ferricyanide is stable; avoid acidification (no pH < ~4). Collect and treat all waste solutions with Fe3+ under alkaline conditions to ensure any cyanide remains complexed as insoluble Prussian blue before disposal.

Expected performance/stability features for alkaline UOR
- Ni-centered active sites (NiOOH) formed at relatively low potentials, further stabilized and activated by Fe; Cu improves urea adsorption and C–N cleavage kinetics; Mn tunes redox balance and defect chemistry; Zn enhances lattice robustness and corrosion resistance.
- Lower UOR onset potential and higher specific activity than binary PBAs (e.g., Ni–Fe) due to synergistic adsorption/reaction pathways and improved charge transport.
- Sustained activity over long-term cycling because the high-entropy matrix resists cation segregation and mitigates destructive Jahn–Teller distortions, while the PBA-to-oxyhydroxide surface reconstruction remains self-limiting and protective.

In sum, the (Ni,Cu,Mn,Zn)–Fe high-entropy Prussian blue analog balances catalytic activity (Ni/Fe/Cu/Mn synergy), structural and corrosion stability (entropy stabilization with Zn as a benign, stable-valence buffer), synthetic simplicity (room-temperature aqueous co-precipitation), low cost (all base metals), and environmental friendliness (no precious/Cr/Co; safe cyanide management) for efficient urea oxidation in alkaline media.
