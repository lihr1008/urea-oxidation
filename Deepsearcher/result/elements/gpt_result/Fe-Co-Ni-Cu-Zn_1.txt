**Executive Summary**

- Proposed high-entropy PBA catalyst: Ni–Co–Fe–Cu–Zn hexacyanoferrate with Fe on the C-coordinated framework site and a quaternary/quinary mix (Ni, Co, Cu, Zn) on the N-coordinated site; minor alkali cations (K+/Na+) to tune vacancies/defects.
- Rationale: Ni and Fe form the proven UOR-active PBA backbone; Co and Cu boost redox density and conductivity; Zn supplies benign, corrosion-resistant, and entropy-stabilizing effects. Mn is intentionally avoided due to Jahn–Teller distortion and dissolution risk under alkaline anodic potentials.
- Targeted outcomes: low UOR overpotential at practical current densities, robust durability (>100 h) in alkaline urea, low-cost salts, simple co-precipitation synthesis, and environmentally safer composition.

**Why Prussian Blue Analogues (PBAs)**

- Green, simple synthesis: Aqueous co‑precipitation at room temperature; scalable and environmentally friendly. Surface/defect engineering (e.g., etching, carbon coating) further enhances performance.
- Structural advantages: Open 3D cyanide-bridged framework with tunable vacancies and interstitial cations; multiple redox-active centers; small volume change “quasi‑zero‑strain” achievable through entropy stabilization.
- Conductivity/stability playbook: Composite with conductive carbon; tune defect/water content; leverage multi‑metal redox synergy; prioritize C‑coordinated Fe for framework integrity and kinetics.

**Metal Selection and Roles**

- Ni (N-site; primary UOR center)
  - Catalytic function: Ni(II) ↔ Ni(III/IV) is central to UOR; in Ni–Fe PBAs, Ni maintains a stable Ni(II) intermediate during initial UOR, correlating with high activity and mitigated deactivation.
  - Stability/corrosion: Stable in alkaline; performance enhanced and stabilized by Fe neighbors and entropy mixing.
  - Synthetic feasibility: Ni readily occupies N‑coordinated sites in PBAs via co‑precipitation.
  - Cost-effectiveness: Abundant and low cost versus noble metals.
  - Structural factors: Ionic radius and octahedral preference fit PBAs; strong synergy with Fe(CN)6 frameworks.

- Fe (C-site; framework and redox)
  - Catalytic function: C‑coordinated Fe redox couples support charge balance and can contribute to capacity while preserving structure; Fe(C) redox maintains phase stability and fast kinetics.
  - Stability/corrosion: Fe(CN)6 units deliver robust frameworks; carbon coordination is less distortion-prone.
  - Synthetic feasibility: Standard hexacyanoferrate precursors yield high-quality PBAs.
  - Cost-effectiveness: Very low-cost, environmentally acceptable.
  - Structural factors: Places the strongest ligand field on the C site; anchors the framework and tolerates multi‑metal disorder.

- Co (N-site; activity and conductivity co‑promoter)
  - Catalytic function: Adds accessible Co(II/III) redox, increases redox‑center density, and improves electronic structure for UOR/OER‑like steps.
  - Stability/corrosion: N‑site Co can be slower kinetically than Fe(C) but remains beneficial in small fractions; entropy mixing mitigates distortions.
  - Synthetic feasibility: Co co‑precipitates with Ni/Fe cyanoferrates cleanly.
  - Cost-effectiveness: Modest cost; use as partial dopant to maintain economy.
  - Structural factors: Octahedral Co(II/III) integrates well; helps suppress Ni isolation and broadens active ensembles.

- Cu (N-site; electronic structure and activity booster)
  - Catalytic function: Cu incorporation in PBAs is known to boost catalytic rates (shown for water splitting PBAs) and tunes band structure to facilitate charge transfer.
  - Stability/corrosion: Cu2+ can be Jahn–Teller active and more prone to leaching under strong anodic conditions; use at low fraction and leverage entropy stabilization to mitigate; embed within carbon composite.
  - Synthetic feasibility: Straightforward co‑precipitation; stable mixed‑metal phases attainable.
  - Cost-effectiveness: Inexpensive, earth‑abundant.
  - Structural factors: Slight JT tendency is buffered by multi‑metal disorder, low loading, and C‑site Fe anchoring; improves conductivity.

- Zn (N-site; benign stabilizer with corrosion resistance)
  - Catalytic function: Redox‑silent or weakly active for UOR, but improves structural stability, suppresses distortions, and moderates local environments to keep Ni active longer.
  - Stability/corrosion: Zn2+ is environmentally benign and enhances corrosion resistance; reported to stabilize high‑entropy PBAs and protect Zn anodes due to favorable interfacial behavior.
  - Synthetic feasibility: Co‑precipitates readily; promotes well‑crystallized frameworks.
  - Cost-effectiveness: Very low cost and low toxicity.
  - Structural factors: D10 Zn2+ in octahedral N‑coordination reduces local JT distortions and vacancy clustering; boosts “quasi‑zero‑strain” behavior collectively.

**Metals Deliberately Avoided**

- Mn: Although active in batteries, Mn3+ exhibits strong Jahn–Teller distortion, structural deactivation, and possible dissolution in alkaline/anodic regimes; codoping can mitigate but adds risk and complexity relative to safer Zn.
- Cr/V: Environmental and toxicity concerns; less desirable for green synthesis and disposal.
- Noble metals (Ru/Ir/Pt): Cost-prohibitive and unnecessary given targeted Ni‑centered UOR.

**Framework and Site-Occupancy Strategy**

- Site assignment: Fe on the C‑coordinated site as [Fe(CN)6] for framework integrity and fast, stable redox; Ni/Co/Cu/Zn on N‑coordinated sites to furnish a high‑entropy, multi‑redox lattice for UOR.
- Cyanide-bridge chemistry: Strong Fe–C≡N–M(N) linkages support charge delocalization and maintain structural coherence under cycling; C‑coordinated Fe reduces distortion relative to N‑coordinated sites.
- Stoichiometry/defects: Use slight alkali filling (K+ preferred) to reduce vacancies and strain; controlled water content to balance porosity and stability; avoid high Mn‑like distortion centers altogether.
- Configurational entropy: Five-metal mixing on the N‑site increases entropy, suppresses order–disorder transitions, reduces volume change (“quasi‑zero‑strain”), and enhances cycling stability; evidence shows Ni and Zn act as stabilizers while Co/Cu contribute capacity/activity.

**Synthesis and Processing**

- Primary route: One‑pot aqueous co‑precipitation of mixed metal salts with potassium hexacyanoferrate at room temperature; tune pH, addition rate, and temperature to control defect density and particle size.
- Disorder control: Introduce high entropy via equimolar or near‑equimolar Ni/Co/Cu/Zn on N‑site; post‑synthetic NH3·H2O etching can create mesoporosity and higher surface area without harming the framework.
- Conductivity enhancement: Composite with conductive carbon (graphene/CNTs/acetylene black) or implement thin carbon coatings to overcome intrinsic low conductivity of PBAs.
- Morphology: Target nanocubes/nanoframes with hollow features to maximize accessible active sites and mass transport, while retaining structural scaffold.

**Performance Targets and Practical Criteria**

- Activity benchmarks: Lower anodic potentials for UOR than OER under identical alkaline conditions; strong currents at modest overpotentials in 1.0 M KOH + urea; outperform same-electrolyte OER baselines. Aim for competitive performance versus state‑of‑the‑art Ni–Fe PBA and derived systems.
- Durability/corrosion resistance: Stable operation over extended chronoamperometry (>100 h) with minimal current decay; limited metal leaching (ICP‑MS); resistance to carbonate/fouling by urea by‑products.
- Cost/environment: Use earth‑abundant salts; no noble metals; aqueous processing; benign Zn as stabilizer; avoid toxic Cr/V.
- Synthesis simplicity: Room‑temperature co‑precipitation; simple washing/drying; optional mild etching and carbon compositing.

**Trade-offs and How They’re Managed**

- Cu JT/distortion vs activity: Use low Cu fraction and entropy buffering; rely on Fe(C) anchoring and carbon compositing to minimize dissolution.
- High entropy vs conductivity: Entropy aids stability; carbon composite and modest Co/Cu content address conductivity.
- Vacancies vs mechanical integrity: Partial K+ filling reduces vacancy‑induced strain while preserving ion transport.

**Validation Plan**

- Structure/entropy
  - XRD and Rietveld refinement to confirm PBA phase and Fe(C) site; assess lattice parameters and “quasi‑zero‑strain” upon cycling.
  - STEM‑EDS mapping to verify uniform multi‑metal distribution; XPS/ICP to confirm stoichiometry.
- Redox/active sites
  - In situ/operando Raman to monitor Ni valence under UOR; verify stabilized Ni(II) intermediate behavior in early UOR.
  - XAS (Fe/Ni/Co/Cu K‑edges) to deconvolute contributions; confirm Fe(C) stability and N‑site redox participation.
- Electrochemistry
  - LSV and Tafel for UOR in 1.0 M KOH + 0.33–1.0 M urea; compare to OER baseline in KOH.
  - Chronoamperometry >100 h; EIS before/after to assess charge transfer changes with carbon composite.
- Corrosion/dissolution
  - ICP‑MS of electrolyte after long holds to quantify Ni/Co/Cu leaching; compare variants with/without Zn.
  - Post‑mortem XRD/TEM to evaluate framework retention.

- General formula: Kx(Ni0.35–0.45Co0.20–0.25Cu0.10–0.15Zn0.20–0.25)[Fe(CN)6]y·zH2O
